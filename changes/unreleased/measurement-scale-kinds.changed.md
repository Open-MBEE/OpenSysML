- A quantity on an `OrdinalScale`, `CyclicRatioScale` or `LogarithmicScale` is now given what its kind defines (SysML v2.1 §9.8.3.2) instead of being refused wholesale. A value on a `CyclicRatioScale` is a magnitude in the scale's `unit`, equivalent over its `modulus`: `450 [bearing] == 90 [bearing]` is `true`, `450 [bearing] + 30 ['°']` is `480 [bearing]`, it scales, negates, sums and converts as its unit does, a set holds one member per cycle class, and the orderings compare places in the cycle counted from zero, so equivalent values are equal (`450 [bearing] < 180 [bearing]`). A value on a `LogarithmicScale` converts and compares through `factor * log_base((x / referenceQuantity) ** exponent)`: `ConvertQuantity(0.1 [W], dBm)` is `20.0 [dBm]` and `30.0 [dBm] == 1.0 [W]` is `true`, a comparison, and `min`/`max`, being made on the left operand's reference; it still has no sum, difference or multiple. A value on an `OrdinalScale` still only compares with values on its own scale, and `ConvertQuantity` to or from it is now refused even where the scale states a `quantityValueMapping`, since one equivalent value gives an ordinal scale no interval. A cyclic scale stating no `modulus`, or a logarithmic one stating no `logarithmBase`, `factor` or `exponent`, is an error where its first value is read. The dimension check no longer warns of arithmetic on a cyclic value, warns of a sum or difference on an ordinal or logarithmic scale, and judges a logarithmic value by the dimension of its `referenceQuantity`. A conversion to or from a `LogarithmicScale` whose result is a Real is made even where the ratio to the `referenceQuantity`, or the power taken on the way, is not: with a reference of `0.001 [W]`, `ConvertQuantity(3100.0 [dBm], W)` is about `1e307 [W]` and `ConvertQuantity(1.0e308 [W], dBm)` is `3110.0 [dBm]`, where both were refused, and a ratio too small for binary64 no longer converts to zero. Converting a value on a scale to a unit that carries it past binary64 is an arithmetic overflow error rather than an infinite quantity. `==` and `!=` against such a value answer `false` and `true` whichever operand is written first; an ordering reports the overflow.
